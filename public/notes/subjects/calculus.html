Limits & Continuity

EXISTENCE OF A LIMIT · STANDARD FORMS · L'HÔPITAL'S RULE · CONTINUITY CONDITIONS

When a limit exists

limx→a f(x) = L exists iff the left-hand limit and right-hand limit both exist and are equal:

lim(x→a⁻) f(x) = lim(x→a⁺) f(x) = L

This one-line check resolves almost every "does the limit exist" GATE question — especially for piecewise functions, |x|-type functions, and functions defined differently on either side of a point.

Standard limit results

LimitValue
lim(x→0) sin(x)/x1
lim(x→0) (1−cos x)/x²1/2

L'Hôpital's rule

For indeterminate forms 0/0 or ∞/∞ only: lim f(x)/g(x) = lim f'(x)/g'(x), provided the latter limit exists. Can be applied repeatedly if the result is still indeterminate.

Other indeterminate forms (0·∞, ∞−∞, 1^∞, 0⁰, ∞⁰) must first be algebraically converted to 0/0 or ∞/∞ before applying L'Hôpital.

Continuity at a point

f(x) is continuous at x = a iff all three conditions hold:

1. f(a) is defined 2. lim(x→a) f(x) exists (LHL = RHL) 3. lim(x→a) f(x) = f(a)
GATE trap

A function can have a limit at a point without being continuous there (removable discontinuity — limit exists but ≠ f(a), or f(a) undefined). Don't conflate "limit exists" with "continuous." These are exactly the two conditions GATE tests as separate MCQ statements.

Practice — GATE style (original)

5 questions
Q1 Evaluate lim(x→0) sin(3x)/x.

Rewrite as 3 · sin(3x)/(3x). As x→0, 3x→0, so sin(3x)/(3x) → 1 by the standard result.

Answer: 3

Q2 Evaluate lim(x→∞) (1 + 3/x)ˣ.

This matches the standard form lim(x→∞)(1+k/x)ˣ = eᵏ, with k=3.

Answer: e³

Q3 Evaluate lim(x→0) (eˣ − 1 − x)/x² using L'Hôpital's rule.

At x=0 this is 0/0. Differentiate top and bottom: lim (eˣ−1)/2x — still 0/0. Differentiate again: lim eˣ/2 = 1/2.

Answer: 1/2

Q4 f(x) = x² for x≠2, and f(2) = 5. Is f continuous at x=2?

lim(x→2) f(x) = lim x² = 4. But f(2) = 5. Since the limit (4) ≠ f(2) (5), condition 3 for continuity fails even though the limit exists.

Answer: Not continuous at x=2 (removable discontinuity)

Q5 Evaluate lim(x→0⁺) x·ln(x).

This is a 0·(−∞) form. Rewrite as ln(x)/(1/x), now −∞/∞, apply L'Hôpital: (1/x)/(−1/x²) = −x → 0.

Answer: 0

Differentiability

DERIVATIVE AS A LIMIT · DIFFERENTIABILITY ⟹ CONTINUITY · STANDARD DERIVATIVES

Definition — derivative as a limit

f'(a) = lim(h→0) [f(a+h) − f(a)] / h

f is differentiable at x=a iff this limit exists — equivalently, iff the left-hand derivative and right-hand derivative both exist and are equal.

The one-way implication GATE builds MCQs around
Differentiable at a point ⟹ Continuous at that point Continuous at a point ⇏ Differentiable at that point

Classic counter-example: f(x) = |x| is continuous everywhere but not differentiable at x=0 (left derivative = −1, right derivative = +1, they disagree).

Product & quotient rules

Chain rule

d/dx [f(g(x))] = f'(g(x)) · g'(x)
GATE trap

A function with a "corner" (like |x|) or a vertical tangent (like x^(1/3) at x=0) is continuous but not differentiable. GATE loves to give a piecewise function and ask you to check both continuity and differentiability at the joining point — always check continuity first, since it's a prerequisite (if it fails, differentiability automatically fails too).

Practice — GATE style (original)

5 questions
Q1 Is f(x) = |x−2| differentiable at x=2?

Left derivative at x=2: for x<2, f(x)=2−x, f'(x)=−1. Right derivative: for x>2, f(x)=x−2, f'(x)=1. Since −1 ≠ 1, the derivative doesn't exist at the corner.

Answer: Not differentiable at x=2 (continuous, but has a corner)

Q2 Find the derivative of f(x) = x²·sin(x) using the product rule.

u=x², v=sin(x). u'=2x, v'=cos(x). (uv)' = u'v+uv' = 2x·sin(x) + x²·cos(x).

Answer: f'(x) = 2x·sin(x) + x²·cos(x)

Q3 Find dy/dx for y = sin(3x²) using the chain rule.

Outer function sin(u) with u=3x². d/dx[sin(u)] = cos(u)·u' = cos(3x²)·6x.

Answer: dy/dx = 6x·cos(3x²)

Q4 If f is differentiable at x=a, what can you conclude about continuity of f at x=a — and is the converse true?

Differentiability always implies continuity at that point. The converse is false — a function can be continuous without being differentiable (e.g., |x| at x=0).

Answer: f is continuous at a; converse does not hold in general

Q5 Find the derivative of f(x) = eˣ/x² using the quotient rule.

u=eˣ, v=x². u'=eˣ, v'=2x. (u/v)' = (u'v−uv')/v² = (eˣ·x² − eˣ·2x)/x⁴ = eˣ(x−2)/x³ after simplifying.

Answer: f'(x) = eˣ(x−2)/x³

03

Mean Value Theorems

ROLLE'S THEOREM · LAGRANGE'S MVT · GEOMETRIC INTERPRETATION

Rolle's theorem

If f is continuous on [a,b], differentiable on (a,b), and f(a) = f(b), then there exists at least one c ∈ (a,b) such that:

f'(c) = 0

Geometric meaning: if a curve starts and ends at the same height, somewhere in between it must have a horizontal tangent.

Lagrange's Mean Value Theorem (LMVT)

If f is continuous on [a,b] and differentiable on (a,b), there exists at least one c ∈ (a,b) such that:

f'(c) = [f(b) − f(a)] / (b − a)

Geometric meaning: somewhere on the curve, the instantaneous slope (tangent) equals the average slope (secant line from a to b). Rolle's theorem is the special case where f(a)=f(b), making the right side zero.

The two hypotheses are both required

Continuity on the closed interval [a,b] AND differentiability on the open interval (a,b) — both are needed. If either fails anywhere in the interval, the theorem's conclusion is not guaranteed (though a value satisfying it might still coincidentally exist).

GATE trap

MVT guarantees existence of at least one c — it says nothing about how many such points exist, and gives no formula for finding c beyond solving f'(c) = (average slope) directly. GATE questions typically ask you to verify the hypotheses hold, then solve for c explicitly.

Practice — GATE style (original)

5 questions
Q1 Verify Rolle's theorem for f(x) = x² − 4x + 3 on [1,3] and find c.

f(1) = 1−4+3 = 0. f(3) = 9−12+3 = 0. f(1)=f(3), continuous & differentiable everywhere (polynomial). f'(x) = 2x−4 = 0 ⇒ x = 2, which lies in (1,3).

Answer: c = 2

Q2 Find c satisfying LMVT for f(x) = x² on [1,4].

Average slope = [f(4)−f(1)]/(4−1) = (16−1)/3 = 5. f'(x) = 2x. Set 2c = 5 ⇒ c = 2.5, which lies in (1,4).

Answer: c = 2.5

Q3 Does Rolle's theorem apply to f(x) = |x| on [−1,1]?

f(−1)=1, f(1)=1, so f(a)=f(b) holds, and f is continuous on [−1,1]. But f is not differentiable at x=0, which lies inside (−1,1) — the differentiability hypothesis fails, so Rolle's theorem does not guarantee a valid c (and indeed f'(x)=0 has no solution here).

Answer: No — differentiability fails at x=0 ∈ (−1,1)

Q4 For f(x) = sin(x) on [0, π], does Rolle's theorem apply, and what is c?

f(0)=0, f(π)=0, continuous and differentiable everywhere. f'(x)=cos(x)=0 ⇒ x=π/2, which lies in (0,π).

Answer: Yes, c = π/2

Q5 f(x) = x³ on [−2, 2]. Find the value(s) of c guaranteed by LMVT.

Average slope = [f(2)−f(−2)]/(2−(−2)) = (8−(−8))/4 = 4. f'(x) = 3x². Set 3c² = 4 ⇒ c² = 4/3 ⇒ c = ±2/√3 ≈ ±1.155. Both values lie within (−2,2), so both are valid.

Answer: c = ±2/√3

04

Maxima & Minima

FIRST & SECOND DERIVATIVE TESTS · CRITICAL POINTS · CLOSED-INTERVAL EXTREMA

Critical points

This is a statement about interior local extrema only. For an interior point c where f is differentiable, Fermat's theorem says: if f has a local max or min at c, then f'(c) = 0. Combined with points where f isn't differentiable at all, the full set of candidates for an interior local extremum is: where f'(x) = 0 (stationary point), or where f'(x) doesn't exist (non-smooth point). These candidates are called critical points.

Critical points alone are not the whole story for absolute extrema on a closed interval [a,b] — an absolute max/min can also occur at an endpoint a or b even where f'≠0 and f' is perfectly well-defined there, simply because the domain doesn't extend further in that direction for the "local" comparison to apply. See the closed-interval method below.

First derivative test

Check the sign of f'(x) just before and just after a critical point c:

f' before cf' after cConclusion at c
+−Local maximum
−+Local minimum
Same sign both sides—Neither a local max nor a local min
"Same sign both sides" only rules out an extremum — it does NOT prove an inflection point

These are two separate questions answered by two different derivatives. "Is c a local extremum?" is answered by whether f' changes sign at c. "Is c an inflection point?" is answered by whether the concavity changes — i.e. whether f'' changes sign at c. These two questions aren't even always asked about the same points: inflection points don't require f'(c)=0 at all. Take f(x) = x + x³: f'(x) = 1 + 3x² > 0 everywhere, so x=0 isn't even a critical point (there's no stationary point to apply the first-derivative table to), yet f''(x) = 6x changes sign at x=0 (concave down for x<0, concave up for x>0) — so x=0 is a genuine inflection point, despite never showing up as a critical point in the first place. Never infer "inflection" just from a critical point having same-sign f' on both sides; always check f'' separately.

Second derivative test — faster when it applies

f'(c) = 0 and f''(c) < 0 ⟹ local maximum at c f'(c) = 0 and f''(c) > 0 ⟹ local minimum at c f'(c) = 0 and f''(c) = 0 ⟹ test is inconclusive — fall back to first derivative test
Global (absolute) extrema on a closed interval

On a closed interval [a,b], evaluate f at: (1) all critical points inside (a,b), and (2) the two endpoints a and b. The largest value found is the absolute maximum, the smallest is the absolute minimum — the endpoints must always be checked, since an extremum can occur there even where f'≠0.

GATE trap

f'(c) = 0 is necessary but not sufficient for an extremum — f(x) = x³ has f'(0) = 0, but f'(x) = 3x² ≥ 0 on both sides (sign never changes), so x=0 is neither a max nor a min. Separately, x=0 also happens to be an inflection point here — but that's established by checking f''(x) = 6x, which changes sign from negative to positive at x=0 (concave down to concave up), not by the same-sign f' observation. The two facts (not-an-extremum, and inflection) need two separate checks even though they land on the same point in this example.

Practice — GATE style (original)

5 questions
Q1 Find and classify the critical points of f(x) = x³ − 3x.

f'(x) = 3x² − 3 = 0 ⇒ x = ±1. f''(x) = 6x. At x=1: f''=6>0 ⇒ local min. At x=−1: f''=−6<0 ⇒ local max.

Answer: Local max at x=−1 (f=2), local min at x=1 (f=−2)

Q2 Find the absolute maximum and minimum of f(x) = x² on [−2, 3].

f'(x)=2x=0 ⇒ x=0 (critical point, f(0)=0). Check endpoints: f(−2)=4, f(3)=9. Comparing {0, 4, 9}: max=9 at x=3, min=0 at x=0.

Answer: Absolute max = 9 (at x=3), absolute min = 0 (at x=0)

Q3 For f(x) = x⁴, show why the second derivative test fails at x=0 and determine the nature of that point another way.

f'(x)=4x³=0 ⇒ x=0. f''(x)=12x², so f''(0)=0 — the test is inconclusive. Using the first derivative test instead: f'(x)=4x³ is negative for x<0 and positive for x>0, so it's a local minimum.

Answer: x=0 is a local minimum (found via first derivative test)

Q4 f(x) = x³ has f'(0) = 0. Is x=0 a local extremum?

f'(x) = 3x² ≥ 0 for all x — it doesn't change sign around x=0 (positive on both sides), so there's no local max or min there. That sign test alone doesn't tell us whether x=0 is an inflection point, though — for that, check f''(x) = 6x separately: it's negative for x<0 and positive for x>0, a genuine sign change, so x=0 is confirmed as an inflection point by the concavity test (f''(0)=0 on its own would only be inconclusive, not sufficient — the sign change either side of it is what actually matters).

Answer: No, x=0 is an inflection point, not an extremum

Q5 Find the maximum value of f(x) = 12x − x³ on the interval [0, 4].

f'(x) = 12 − 3x² = 0 ⇒ x² = 4 ⇒ x = ±2. Only x=2 lies in [0,4]. f''(x) = −6x, f''(2) = −12 < 0 ⇒ local max. Compare f(2)=24−8=16 against endpoints f(0)=0, f(4)=48−64=−16.

Answer: Maximum value = 16 at x = 2

STANDARD INTEGRALS · SUBSTITUTION & BY-PARTS · DEFINITE INTEGRAL PROPERTIES

Standard integrals

f(x)∫f(x)dx

1/xln|x| + C

sin(x)−cos(x) + C

Integration by substitution

When the integrand contains g(x) and g'(x) together, set u = g(x), du = g'(x)dx, and rewrite the integral entirely in terms of u.

Integration by parts
∫u dv = uv − ∫v du

Choose u using LIATE priority (Log, Inverse trig, Algebraic, Trig, Exponential) — pick u as the type earliest in this list.

Definite integral properties

∫ₐᵇ f(x)dx = −∫ᵦᵃ f(x)dx ∫ₐᵃ f(x)dx = 0 ∫₀ᵃ f(x)dx = ∫₀ᵃ f(a−x)dx (the "King's property" — extremely common GATE shortcut)
Even/odd function shortcut

∫₋ₐᵃ f(x)dx = 2∫₀ᵃ f(x)dx if f is even (f(−x)=f(x)); = 0 if f is odd (f(−x)=−f(x)). Recognizing symmetry instantly can skip the entire computation.

GATE trap

Improper integrals (infinite limits, or an integrand blowing up inside the interval) must be evaluated as a limit — e.g. ∫₁^∞ f(x)dx = lim(t→∞) ∫₁ᵗ f(x)dx. The integral converges only if this limit exists (is finite); otherwise it diverges. GATE often tests convergence/divergence of ∫ 1/xᵖ style integrals — converges for p>1, diverges for p≤1 (on [1,∞)).

Practice — GATE style (original)

5 questions
Q1 Evaluate ∫ x·eˣ dx using integration by parts.

By LIATE, u=x (algebraic), dv=eˣdx. du=dx, v=eˣ. ∫u dv = uv−∫v du = x·eˣ − ∫eˣdx = x·eˣ − eˣ + C.

Answer: (x−1)eˣ + C

Q2 Evaluate ∫₀^(π/2) sin(x)/(sin x + cos x) dx using the King's property.

Let I = ∫₀^(π/2) sin(x)/(sinx+cosx)dx. By King's property (a=π/2), I = ∫₀^(π/2) cos(x)/(sinx+cosx)dx also. Adding both expressions of I: 2I = ∫₀^(π/2) 1 dx = π/2.

Answer: I = π/4

Q3 Evaluate ∫₋₂² x³ dx without computing the antiderivative, using symmetry.

f(x)=x³ is odd (f(−x) = −x³ = −f(x)). The integral of an odd function over a symmetric interval [−a,a] is always 0.

Answer: 0

Q4 Evaluate ∫ 2x·cos(x²) dx using substitution.

Let u = x², du = 2x dx. The integral becomes ∫cos(u)du = sin(u) + C = sin(x²) + C.

Answer: sin(x²) + C

Q5 Does ∫₁^∞ 1/x² dx converge or diverge? Find its value if it converges.

This is the 1/xᵖ form with p=2 > 1, so it converges. Compute: lim(t→∞) ∫₁ᵗ x⁻²dx = lim(t→∞) [−1/x]₁ᵗ = lim(t→∞) (−1/t + 1) = 1.

Answer: Converges, value = 1

Revision Sheet

EVERYTHING ON ONE SCREEN — LAST-MINUTE PASS
Limit existence

lim exists iff LHL = RHL. Standard: sinx/x→1, (1−cosx)/x²→1/2, (eˣ−1)/x→1, (1+1/x)ˣ→e.

L'Hôpital's rule

Only for 0/0 or ∞/∞: lim f/g = lim f'/g'. Convert other indeterminate forms first.

Continuity (3 conditions)

f(a) defined, lim(x→a)f(x) exists, and lim = f(a). All three required.

Differentiability

Differentiable ⟹ continuous (one-way only). |x| at 0: continuous, not differentiable.

Rolle's theorem

f(a)=f(b), continuous [a,b], differentiable (a,b) ⟹ ∃c: f'(c)=0.

LMVT

∃c ∈(a,b): f'(c) = [f(b)−f(a)]/(b−a). Rolle's is the special case f(a)=f(b).

Extremum tests

f'(c)=0 necessary, not sufficient. 2nd derivative test: f''<0 ⇒ max, f''>0 ⇒ min, f''=0 ⇒ inconclusive (use 1st derivative test).

Closed-interval extrema

Check all interior critical points AND both endpoints — largest/smallest value found wins.

Integration by parts

∫u dv = uv − ∫v du. Choose u by LIATE: Log, Inverse trig, Algebraic, Trig, Exponential.

King's property

∫₀ᵃ f(x)dx = ∫₀ᵃ f(a−x)dx. Very common trick for definite trig integrals.

Even/odd shortcut

∫₋ₐᵃ f dx = 2∫₀ᵃ f dx if even; = 0 if odd. Check symmetry before integrating directly.

Improper integral convergence

∫₁^∞ 1/xᵖ dx converges iff p > 1. Evaluate as a limit of a proper integral.

Practice — rapid-fire mix (original)

5 questions
Q1 Evaluate lim(x→0) (tan x − x)/x³.

0/0 form. Apply L'Hôpital: lim (sec²x − 1)/3x² = lim tan²x/3x² (since sec²x−1=tan²x) = lim (tanx/x)² / 3 = 1/3.

Answer: 1/3

Q2 A function is differentiable everywhere on (a,b) and continuous on [a,b] with f(a)=f(b)=5. What must exist somewhere in (a,b)?

Rolle's theorem hypotheses are satisfied (continuity on closed interval, differentiability on open interval, equal endpoint values), so there must exist at least one c ∈ (a,b) with f'(c) = 0.

Answer: A point c where f'(c) = 0

Q3 f(x) = x² − 6x + 5. Find its minimum value and where it occurs.

f'(x) = 2x − 6 = 0 ⇒ x = 3. f''(x) = 2 > 0 ⇒ local min. f(3) = 9 − 18 + 5 = −4.

Answer: Minimum value = −4 at x = 3

Q4 Evaluate ∫₀^π sin(x) dx.

∫sin(x)dx = −cos(x). Evaluate from 0 to π: [−cos(π)] − [−cos(0)] = −(−1) − (−1) = 1 + 1.

Answer: 2

Q5 Is f(x) = x^(1/3) continuous and differentiable at x = 0?

f is continuous at x=0 (lim x^(1/3) = 0 = f(0)). But f'(x) = (1/3)x^(−2/3), which → ∞ as x→0 — the tangent becomes vertical, so the derivative does not exist (finitely) at x=0.

Answer: Continuous but not differentiable at x=0 (vertical tangent)
