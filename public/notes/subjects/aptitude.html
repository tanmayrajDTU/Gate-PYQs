15 marks that are almost free if you drill them right.

General Aptitude is common to every GATE paper and worth 15 of the 100 marks — Verbal, Numerical/Data Interpretation, Analytical, and Spatial Aptitude. None of it needs new theory beyond school-level English and maths; it rewards speed, pattern recognition, and not falling for the wording traps GATE builds in. Six sheets, worked examples, and the exact traps to watch for.

01

Verbal Aptitude

GRAMMAR · VOCABULARY · READING COMPREHENSION · NARRATIVE SEQUENCING

Grammar — the recurring GATE error types

GATE's "error spotting" and "fill in the blank" questions cycle through a small set of grammar traps every year. Learn to spot these six and you cover most of the grammar marks:

Error typeWhat to check
Subject–verb agreementSingular/plural match, ignore intervening phrases
Preposition usageFixed pairs — "married to", "different from", "consists of"
Tense consistencyDon't switch tense mid-sentence without a time cue
Articles (a/an/the)"a" before consonant sound, "an" before vowel sound
ParallelismItems in a list/comparison must share grammatical form
Modifier placementModifier must sit next to the word it describes
Subject–verb agreement trap

GATE loves to bury the real subject behind a prepositional phrase: "The list of items is long" — not "are" — because the subject is "list" (singular), not "items". Cross out the phrase between subject and verb before deciding.

Vocabulary — words, idioms, phrases in context

Two question styles dominate: pick the word closest in meaning (synonym) or opposite in meaning (antonym) to a given word, and complete a sentence with the option that fits the idiom or contextual meaning. Build recognition rather than rote lists — read the sentence twice, decide the tone (positive/negative/neutral) the blank needs, then eliminate options that clash with that tone before checking exact meaning.

Common confusable pairs

affect/effect, imply/infer, compliment/complement, stationary/stationery, principal/principle, elicit/illicit — GATE tests these directly as "choose the correct word."

Idiom-in-context strategy

Don't translate literally. "Once in a blue moon" = rarely; "cut corners" = do something poorly to save time/cost. Match the idiom's real-world meaning to the sentence's logic, not its individual words.

Reading comprehension

Short passage (usually 100–250 words) followed by 1–2 questions on the main idea, an inference, or the author's tone/attitude. Read the questions first, then the passage — this tells you what to look for and saves a full re-read. Distinguish three answer types:

  • Stated fact — answer is directly in the text, just paraphrased.
  • Inference — answer follows logically from the text but isn't stated word-for-word; avoid options that go beyond what the passage supports.
  • Tone/attitude — judged from word choice (critical, neutral, appreciative, skeptical), not from the topic itself.
GATE trap

The most common wrong option in RC questions is "true in the real world but not supported by this specific passage." GATE RC answers must be justifiable using only the given text — outside knowledge, however correct, is not a valid basis for choosing an option.

Narrative sequencing

Four or five jumbled sentences must be arranged into a coherent paragraph. Find the sentence that introduces the topic (usually has no pronoun referring backward), then chain the rest using pronoun references, connecting words (however, therefore, meanwhile), and logical/chronological order.

Sequencing shortcut

Scan for pronouns (it, this, they) and connectors (but, so, thus) first — a sentence starting with one of these almost never opens the paragraph, which quickly eliminates 1–2 of the 4–5 ordering options.

Practice — GATE style (original)

5 questions
Q1 Choose the correct sentence: (a) "Neither of the answers are correct." (b) "Neither of the answers is correct."

In this sentence, "neither" is treated as singular, so the verb is "is," regardless of the plural noun phrase that follows it ("of the answers") — the verb must agree with "neither," not "answers."

Answer: (b) "Neither of the answers is correct."

Q2 Fill in the blank: "The committee ___ its decision yesterday." Choose between "announced" and "have announced".

"Announced" is required because "yesterday" signals simple past tense, not present perfect ("have announced" would need a present-perfect context, not a specific past time marker like "yesterday"). "Committee" is also being treated as a singular unit here — though collective nouns aren't universally singular across English varieties; British English commonly permits plural agreement when the group is being treated as individual members rather than one unit.

Answer: "announced"

Q3 Pick the word closest in meaning to "frugal."

"Frugal" means economical or careful with money/resources, avoiding waste — closest to "thrifty," not "wealthy" or "generous," which are common distractor options.

Answer: Thrifty

Q4 A passage states research shows a correlation between two variables but never claims causation. Which conclusion is safe to pick as the answer?

Any option asserting that one variable "causes" the other goes beyond what the passage supports — correlation-only text supports only a correlation-based conclusion.

Answer: The conclusion that states correlation only, not causation

Q5 Arrange into a coherent paragraph: (P) However, the results were inconclusive. (Q) Scientists ran the experiment three times. (R) This raised further questions. (S) They wanted to confirm the initial finding.

Q introduces the topic with no backward pronoun. S explains why (uses "they" referring to scientists). P contrasts with "however." R follows P using "this" to refer to the inconclusive result.

Answer: Q – S – P – R

02

Numerical & Data Interpretation

RATIOS · PERCENTAGES · POWERS, EXPONENTS & LOGARITHMS · DATA INTERPRETATION

Ratios, percentages, and the "shortcut vs setup" decision

Almost every numerical-computation question is solvable two ways: set up an equation, or use a ratio/percentage shortcut. GATE's 1-mark time budget (~1 minute) rewards the shortcut — but only once you trust it, so practice the equation method first to build intuition, then compress to the shortcut.

Percentage change chaining
Net % change (two successive changes a%, b%) = a + b + (a·b)/100

Works for combinations of increase/decrease — use negative values for decreases. E.g. +20% then −10% → 20 − 10 − 2 = +8% net, not +10%.

Ratio scaling
A:B = 2:3, B:C = 4:5 ⇒ A:B:C = (2×4):(3×4):(3×5) = 8:12:15

Scale each ratio so the shared term (B) matches before combining.

GATE trap

logₐ(m+n) is not logₐm + logₐn — that identity only applies to products, never to sums. This is the single most common log-identity mistake GATE options are designed to catch.

Data interpretation — graphs, plots, maps, tables

DI questions give a bar graph, pie chart, line plot, 2D/3D plot, map, or table and ask 1–2 questions requiring you to read values and compute a ratio, percentage, or difference. The data itself is rarely tricky — the time cost is in reading the chart correctly.

  • Pie charts: convert the needed slice's percentage or angle to an absolute value first (angle/360 × total, or %/100 × total) before comparing across slices.
  • Bar/line graphs with two axes: check units on each axis separately — GATE sometimes pairs a left axis in one unit with a right axis in another.
  • Tables: scan the row/column headers fully before reading values — misreading which row is "2024" vs "2025" is the most common self-inflicted error.
Estimation over precision

DI options are usually spread far enough apart that rounding to 1–2 significant figures gets you to the right choice faster than exact computation — don't multiply out exact decimals when an estimate clearly separates the options.

Practice — GATE style (original)

5 questions
Q1 A price is increased by 25% and then decreased by 20%. What is the net percentage change?

Net change = a + b + ab/100 = 25 + (−20) + (25×−20)/100 = 25 − 20 − 5 = 0.

Answer: 0% (no net change)

Q2 If A:B = 3:4 and B:C = 8:9, find A:B:C.

Scale so B matches: A:B = 3:4 = 6:8 (×2). Now B:C = 8:9 already has B=8. So A:B:C = 6:8:9.

Answer: 6 : 8 : 9

Q3 Simplify log₂(32) − log₂(4).

logₐ(m/n) = logₐm − logₐn, so this equals log₂(32/4) = log₂(8) = 3 (since 2³=8).

Answer: 3

Q4 A pie chart shows "Transport" as a 90° slice of a total budget of ₹2,40,000. What is the transport spend?

Fraction of circle = 90/360 = 1/4. Spend = (1/4) × 2,40,000 = 60,000.

Answer: ₹60,000

Q5 A quantity grows from 200 to 250. What percentage increase is this?

% increase = (change/original) × 100 = (50/200) × 100 = 25%.

Answer: 25%

Permutations, Mensuration & Probability

PERMUTATIONS & COMBINATIONS · MENSURATION & BASIC GEOMETRY · ELEMENTARY PROBABILITY & STATISTICS

Permutations vs combinations — the one question that decides everything

Before touching a formula, ask: does order matter? Arranging (order matters) → permutation. Selecting/grouping (order doesn't matter) → combination. Almost every GATE P&C mistake comes from skipping this check.

nPr = n! / (n−r)! — arrangements of r items from n nCr = n! / (r! (n−r)!) — selections of r items from n nCr = nPr / r!
Circular arrangement

n distinct people around a circle: (n−1)! arrangements (fixing one person removes rotational duplicates). If clockwise and counter-clockwise are considered identical (e.g. beads on a necklace), divide by 2 as well: (n−1)!/2.

Elementary probability & statistics

P(event) = (favourable outcomes) / (total outcomes) P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B) P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) — only if A, B independent

Mean, median, mode

Mean = sum/count. Median = middle value when sorted (average of two middles if n is even). Mode = most frequent value. GATE often asks which measure is least affected by outliers — the median.

Complement trick

"At least one" probability questions are almost always faster via the complement: P(at least one) = 1 − P(none).

GATE trap

"Selecting a committee of 3 from 5 men and 4 women" is a combination problem (order doesn't matter within the committee) — but if the question instead asks to assign 3 distinct roles (President, Secretary, Treasurer) to 3 of those people, it becomes a permutation. Re-read the question stem for "arrange/assign roles" vs "select/choose a group" every time.

Practice — GATE style (original)

5 questions
Q1 In how many ways can 3 books be chosen from 7 distinct books?

Order doesn't matter (a selection, not an arrangement) → combination. 7C3 = 7!/(3!4!) = (7×6×5)/(3×2×1) = 35.

Answer: 35

Q2 6 people are to be seated around a circular table. How many distinct arrangements are possible?

Circular arrangement of n distinct items = (n−1)!. Here n=6, so 5! = 120.

Answer: 120

Q3 A cylindrical tank has radius 7 m and height 10 m. Find its volume. (use π ≈ 22/7)

Volume = πr²h = (22/7) × 7² × 10 = (22/7) × 49 × 10 = 22 × 7 × 10 = 1540.

Answer: 1540 m³

Q4 Two fair coins are tossed. What is the probability of getting at least one head?

Use the complement: P(at least one head) = 1 − P(no heads) = 1 − P(both tails) = 1 − (1/2×1/2) = 1 − 1/4.

Answer: 3/4

Q5 Data set: 4, 8, 6, 5, 3, 8, 9. Find the median.

Sort: 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 8, 9 (7 values). The median is the 4th (middle) value.

Answer: 6

Analytical Aptitude

LOGIC: DEDUCTION & INDUCTION · ANALOGY · NUMERICAL RELATIONS & REASONING

Deduction — statements & conclusions

Given one or more statements, decide which conclusion(s) necessarily follow using only formal logic — not real-world plausibility. This is the section where "sounds true" and "logically follows" diverge most often, and GATE options are built specifically to separate the two.

Syllogism validity — the only thing that matters

A conclusion is valid only if it follows from the given premises by strict logical necessity, regardless of whether it matches real-world fact. "All roses are flowers. Some flowers fade quickly." does NOT let you conclude "some roses fade quickly" — the middle term ("flowers") isn't distributed correctly across both premises.

Statement formValid conclusion pattern
All A are B; All B are CAll A are C
No A are B; All C are ANo C are B
Some A are B; All B are CSome A are C
All A are B; No B are CNo A are C

Induction — pattern & sequence completion

Given a sequence of numbers, letters, or figures, identify the underlying rule (arithmetic step, geometric ratio, alternating pattern, digit manipulation) and extend it. Check differences, then ratios, then "difference of differences," in that order, before assuming an irregular pattern.

2, 6, 12, 20, 30, ? Differences: 4, 6, 8, 10, 12 → each difference itself increases by 2 Next term = 30 + 12 = 42

Analogy

A:B :: C:? — first name the exact relationship between A and B in one precise phrase (not just "related to"), then find the option that shares that identical relationship with C. Common relationship types: part-to-whole, cause-to-effect, tool-to-user, degree/intensity, category membership.

GATE trap

Analogy distractors often share a surface-level theme with A:B but not the precise relationship. "Doctor:Hospital :: Teacher:?" — the relationship is "person:workplace," so "School" is correct; "Student" shares the theme (education) but is the wrong relationship type (person:person, not person:place).

Numerical relations & reasoning

Blood relations, direction sense, seating arrangements, and coded relationships. Draw a quick diagram (family tree, compass directions, seating row/circle) rather than tracking relationships in your head — this is the single biggest speed and accuracy lever in this sub-section.

Practice — GATE style (original)

5 questions
Q1 "All engineers are problem-solvers. All problem-solvers are analytical." What follows?

This is a valid "All A are B; All B are C ⟹ All A are C" chain — the middle term "problem-solvers" is fully distributed in both statements.

Answer: All engineers are analytical

Q2 Find the next number: 3, 8, 15, 24, 35, ?

Differences: 5, 7, 9, 11 — each increases by 2, so the next difference is 13. Next term = 35 + 13 = 48. (Note: also fits n² + 2n, confirming the pattern.)

Answer: 48

Q3 Complete the analogy: Pen : Write :: Knife : ?

The relationship is tool:function-it-performs. A knife's core function is to cut.

Answer: Cut

Q4 Pointing to a photograph, a man says "She is the daughter of my grandfather's only son." How is the woman related to the man?

Grandfather's only son = the man's father (since it's his own grandfather). Daughter of the man's father = the man's sister.

Answer: Sister

Q5 A person walks 5 km North, then 3 km East, then 5 km South. How far is he from the starting point, and in which direction?

The North and South legs cancel out (5 km each, opposite directions), leaving only the 3 km East displacement.

Answer: 3 km, East

Spatial Aptitude

TRANSFORMATION OF SHAPES · PAPER FOLDING & CUTTING · ASSEMBLING & GROUPING · 2D/3D VISUALIZATION

Transformation of shapes

Translation (slide), rotation (turn about a point), mirroring/reflection (flip about a line), and scaling (resize) — GATE shows a shape, applies one or more transformations, and asks which option matches the result. Track transformations one at a time rather than trying to visualize the combined result in one step.

Mirror vs rotation — the fastest way to tell them apart

A 180° rotation and a mirror reflection can look similar at a glance. Test with an asymmetric feature (a dot in one corner, a notch on one edge): rotation moves it to the diagonally opposite position keeping the same "handedness"; mirroring flips handedness (left-right reverses) as if seen in a mirror.

Rotation angle check

For rotational-symmetry questions, count how many identical positions a shape passes through in one full 360° turn — that count tells you the smallest rotation angle that maps the shape onto itself (360°/count).

Paper folding & cutting

A square/rectangular paper is folded one or more times, then a cut or hole-punch is made, and you must predict the unfolded pattern. Work backwards from the last fold: unfold one step at a time, mirroring the cut across each fold line as you go, rather than trying to picture the fully unfolded sheet in one jump.

Symmetry rule for folded cuts

Every fold line becomes an axis of symmetry in the final unfolded pattern. If a paper is folded twice (into quarters) and a single hole is punched, the unfolded sheet will show 4 holes arranged with mirror symmetry across both fold lines.

Assembling & grouping

Given several 2D pieces, identify whether they can assemble into a target shape (jigsaw-style), or given a set of 3D-looking objects/patterns, group them by a shared visual rule. Check piece edges for matching length and angle before checking orientation — mismatched edges eliminate options fastest.

2D/3D visualization

Classic question types: identify which 3D solid results from folding a given 2D net (unfolded surface pattern), or which of several answer choices could NOT be a rotation of a given 3D cube/solid.

GATE trap

For "which cube can be formed by folding this net" questions, faces that are adjacent in the 2D net are also adjacent in the 3D cube — but faces that end up opposite each other in the cube are usually NOT adjacent in the net (for a standard cross-shaped net, opposite-face pairs are separated by exactly one square in a straight line of four).

Practice — GATE style (original)

5 questions
Q1 A shape has 5-fold rotational symmetry (like a regular pentagon). What is the smallest angle it can be rotated by and still look identical?

5-fold symmetry means the shape maps onto itself at 5 equally spaced positions in a full 360° turn: 360°/5.

Answer: 72°

Q2 A square sheet of paper is folded once in half (bottom to top), then a single hole is punched through both layers near the folded edge. How many holes appear when unfolded?

One fold means two layers were punched through together, so unfolding reveals both holes, mirrored across the single fold line.

Answer: 2 holes

Q3 An "F"-shaped figure is rotated 180°. Compared to a mirror image of the same "F", how do the two results differ?

A 180° rotation preserves handedness (the "F" is upside-down but not flipped left-right), while a mirror reflection reverses handedness (left-right flipped) — the two results are visibly different from each other.

Answer: The rotated figure is upside-down but not left-right reversed; the mirrored figure is left-right reversed

Q4 The net below folds into a cube. If face C is the "front," which labeled face becomes the "back" (opposite) face? 
        B
    A   C   D   E
        F
(A central square C has B, F, A, D attached to its top/bottom/left/right respectively; E is attached to the right of D, extending that arm into a straight run of four: A–C–D–E.)

The four squares A, C, D, E form a straight run that folds into a closed loop of side faces (front/right/back/left). With C as front, its immediate neighbors in the strip (A and D) become the left and right faces, and the face two steps away in the loop — E — wraps around to become the back, directly opposite C. (B and F, attached above/below C, become the top and bottom faces, opposite each other.)

Answer: E is opposite C (back face); B and F are the other opposite pair (top/bottom)

Q5 Two straight edges (from separate pieces of a larger geometric assembly) are required to coincide completely along the same boundary segment — i.e. fully overlap, not just meet end-to-end to jointly span a longer run. What necessary condition must their lengths satisfy?

For two straight edges to coincide completely along the same boundary segment (fully overlapping, not just meeting end-to-end to jointly span a longer run), equal length is a necessary (not sufficient) condition — edges of different lengths simply cannot fully overlap. Note that if the edges were instead allowed to sit end-to-end (e.g. a 4 cm edge followed by a 3 cm edge forming a 7 cm run), equal length would not be required at all — that's a different geometric relationship. Equal length alone doesn't guarantee the edges actually coincide in a real assembly; it's necessary but not sufficient, since other geometric factors (position, orientation, profile) also have to match.

Answer: The two edges must have equal length (necessary, not sufficient, condition)

Revision Sheet

EVERYTHING ON ONE SCREEN — LAST-MINUTE PASS
Subject–verb agreement

Cross out phrases between subject and verb — the verb agrees with the real subject only. "Neither/either" is always singular.

RC answer discipline

Only what the passage directly states or strictly implies counts — outside knowledge, however true, is not a valid basis for the answer.

Successive % change

Net % change = a + b + ab/100 (use negative values for decreases). Never just add a and b.

Log identities

log(mn)=log m+log n; log(m/n)=log m−log n; log(mⁿ)=n·log m. log(m+n) has NO simplification.

Permutation vs combination

Order matters (arrange/assign roles) ⟹ nPr. Order doesn't matter (select/choose) ⟹ nCr = nPr/r!.

Circular arrangement

n distinct items around a circle: (n−1)! arrangements. Divide by 2 more if clockwise/counter-clockwise are identical.

"At least one" probability

Always faster via the complement: P(at least one) = 1 − P(none).

Syllogism validity

A conclusion is valid only if it follows by strict logical necessity from the premises — real-world truth is irrelevant to validity.

Sequence patterns

Check in order: constant difference → constant ratio → difference-of-differences → digit/positional manipulation.

Rotation vs mirror

Rotation preserves handedness (turns in place); mirroring reverses handedness (left-right flips). Test with an asymmetric feature.

Folded-paper symmetry

Every fold line becomes an axis of symmetry in the unfolded result — n folds through a punch point can multiply hole count by up to 2ⁿ.

Cube net opposite faces

In a straight line of four squares in a net, the first and last square become opposite faces once folded into a cube.

How to use this sheet

Read sections 01–05 once for understanding, then loop back to this page alone for every later revision pass — if any card doesn't immediately make sense, that's the section to reopen. General Aptitude rewards drilling speed under time pressure more than depth of theory — once the rules above are automatic, the main gain left is timed practice sets.

Practice — rapid-fire mix (original)

5 questions
Q1 Choose the correct option: "Each of the students ___ given a certificate." (has / have)

"Each" is always singular regardless of the plural noun that follows it ("of the students").

Answer: has

Q2 A shirt's price is discounted by 30% and then by an additional 10% on the discounted price. What is the overall discount?

Net change = a + b + ab/100 with a=−30, b=−10: −30 − 10 + (−30×−10)/100 = −40 + 3 = −37.

Answer: 37% overall discount

Q3 How many distinct 4-letter arrangements can be made from the letters of the word "GATE" (no repeats)?

4 distinct letters, arranging all 4: 4P4 = 4! = 24.

Answer: 24

Q4 "All squares are rectangles. This shape is a rectangle." Can you validly conclude "this shape is a square"?

No — the premise only tells us squares are a subset of rectangles, not that all rectangles are squares. Concluding "square" from "rectangle" reverses the valid direction of the implication.

Answer: No, that conclusion is not valid

Q5 Find the next term: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, ?

These are perfect squares: 1², 2², 3², 4², 5² — next is 6².

Answer: 36
